\section{Protocols for the public-fleet tests}
\label{sec:protocols}

Every number in Table~\ref{M-tab:fleets} is recomputed by
\texttt{verify\_numbers.py} in the repository named in the main text, and the
parameters below are those passed by that script; where a test was also run
at other settings to check stability, both are given. Every test below
that uses an informative direction estimates it as a time derivative of the
monitoring channels along normalised life, and therefore relies on the
time-shift population of Section~\ref{M-sec:model}, under which that
derivative is the sensitivity of Eq.~\eqref{M-eq:sens}.

\subsection{Rotation test on the Severson cells}

The channels are discharge capacity, internal resistance and mean cell
temperature. A cell is used if its life is recorded, it has at least $400$
cycles, and all three channels are finite throughout; $129$ cells qualify.
Life is normalised per cell to $\tau\in[0,1]$ by cycle index. Each channel is
differentiated with a Savitzky--Golay filter of polynomial order $3$ and
window $\min(301,\,2\lfloor n/4\rfloor+1)$ cycles for a cell of $n$ cycles,
and the derivative is divided by the standard deviation of that channel's
residual about the same filter, which whitens the channel by its own measured
noise. The whitened derivative is interpolated onto $40$ points of
$\tau\in[0.15,0.90]$. One sign per channel, the median sign over every cell
and grid point, orients all cells consistently; each cell's vector is then
normalised at every grid point, and the renormalised fleet mean is the
empirical informative direction. Its arc is the sum of the angles between
consecutive grid points, and its end-to-end angle is the angle between the
first and the last. Positivity is the fraction of grid points at which every
component of the fleet mean is positive; it equals one at the window used.

\subsection{Remaining-life test on the Severson cells}

The test uses the cells, smoothing, whitening and orientation of the rotation
test and is run leave-one-out: for each test cell, the reference path, the
noise levels and the fixed indicator are computed from the other $128$ cells.
Each channel is referred to its own baseline, the median of its first $50$
cycles. The reference path is the median over cells of the smoothed,
baseline-referred channels against normalised life, tabulated at $1001$
points, and the noise level of each channel is the median over cells of its
residual standard deviation. The fixed indicator is the renormalised mean
over $\tau\in[0.15,0.90]$ of the fleet-mean informative direction. At an
elapsed cycle count $n_0$, the integer nearest to $\tau_0(n-1)$ for a cell of
$n$ cycles with $\tau_0\in\{0.5,0.7,0.9\}$, the estimator observes the cycles
from $0.7n_0$ to $n_0$ and returns the life $L$, searched over the integers
from $n_0+1$ to $4000$, for which the reference path evaluated at each
observed cycle divided by $L-1$ is closest to the observations in whitened
least squares; the error is $(L-n)/n$. The estimator is run on the three
whitened channels, on the fixed indicator, on capacity alone, on temperature
alone and on a fusion indicator, and each single-signal estimator is compared
with the three-channel one through the ratio of their mean squared errors
over cells. The fusion weights are $C^{-1}\bar\Delta$, normalised to unit
length, where $\bar\Delta$ and $C$ are the mean and the between-cell
covariance, over the training cells, of the whitened, oriented,
baseline-referred smoothed channels at failure; they make the indicator's
value at failure as consistent between cells as possible relative to its mean
change over life, the criterion of data-level
fusion~\cite{liu2013fusion}.
Observing the last $20\%$ or $50\%$ of the elapsed cycles instead, or taking
the baseline over the first $20$ or $100$ cycles, keeps that ratio, pooled
over the three ages, between $0.95$ and $0.97$ for the fixed indicator
and between $1.03$ and $1.06$ for capacity alone.

\subsection{Replacement policy on the Severson cells}

Each cell is inspected every $25$ cycles from cycle $100$ until its end of
life. At each inspection the estimator of the remaining-life test, with the
life searched from $n_0+1$ to $n_0+1500$, predicts the remaining life, and the
cell is replaced at the first inspection at which the prediction is at most a
threshold $\theta$; a cell not replaced before its end of life fails. With
$r$ the ratio of the corrective to the preventive cost, the long-run cost per
cycle is $(r\,n_{\mathrm f}+n_{\mathrm p})/\sum_kT_k$, where $n_{\mathrm f}$
and $n_{\mathrm p}$ count failures and preventive replacements and $T_k$ is
the service time of cell $k$. The threshold is chosen on a grid from $0$ to
$1000$ cycles in steps of $5$ to minimise this cost, separately for each
signal. At $r=10$ the optimal threshold is $130$ cycles for all three
channels, the fixed indicator and capacity alone, and $120$ cycles for the
fusion indicator. At the smallest threshold that keeps failures at or below
$5\%$, which is $90$ cycles for every signal, the mean unused life is $92$
cycles for all three channels, the fixed indicator and capacity alone, and
$112$ cycles for the fusion indicator.

\subsection{Measured fleets that do not permit a test of actuation}

A test of actuation requires the operating point to vary within a unit while
all other conditions are held constant. In XJTU-SY each of three operating
conditions is applied to its own five bearings, and in PRONOSTIA each of three
conditions to its own bearings, so a difference between conditions cannot be
distinguished from a difference between groups of units. The NASA battery
fleet has four cells. These fleets are therefore listed with the reason and
without a statistic.

\subsection{Regime contrast on C-MAPSS}

The training files of FD004 and FD002 are used. Life is normalised per engine
by its final cycle. Operating regimes are labelled by standardising the three
operating settings over the file and rounding to one decimal; each file has
$6$ regimes. A sensor is retained if its standard deviation within an
(engine, regime) group has a positive median, and each retained sensor is
standardised within each regime, so that the level of a regime does not enter
the direction.

Within one engine, the cycles of each regime are resampled to a common count
$m$, the smallest over that engine's regimes, and an engine enters only if
$m\ge2(w+3)$. Each resampled series is split into its even and its odd
cycles, two half-samples of the same regime. On each half the direction is
estimated as for the Severson cells, with polynomial order $2$ and window $w$,
and interpolated onto $10$ points of $\tau\in[0.30,0.85]$. The angle between
two directions is the mean over grid points of the pointwise angle. $W$ is the
mean, over the engine's regimes, of the angle between the two halves of the
same regime, and $B$ the mean, over pairs of different regimes, of the angle
between a half of one and a half of the other. $B-W$ is positive when regimes
differ by more than half-samples of one regime differ from each other, as
predicted by an operating point that moves the observed direction. The
contrast does not, however, indicate how the direction is moved. C-MAPSS
imposes its flow and efficiency losses as functions of time and lets the
regime act only on the thermodynamic response of the
sensors~\cite{saxena2008}, so a positive contrast there reflects the
observation map, not the stress factor of Eq.~\eqref{M-eq:plant}, and cannot
test actuation. The table reports the mean of $B-W$ over engines, and the
number of engines for which it is positive, at $w=7$; at $w=9$ and $w=11$
every value keeps its sign and exceeds the largest control bias.

The negative control applies the same procedure to surrogate regimes. Within
one regime of one engine with at least $4(w+3)$ cycles, the four interleaved
subsequences of every fourth cycle serve as four surrogate regimes; $W$
compares the first with the second and $B$ the first with the fourth. No
contrast exists by construction. Over all three windows and both files the
mean of $B-W$ stays within $0.5^{\circ}$ of zero, and a paired $t$ test of
$B$ against $W$ does not reject equality at any window, the smallest $p$
being $0.35$.

\subsection{Joint test of separability and channel pairing on N-CMAPSS}

The development sets of DS02, units $2$, $5$, $10$, $16$, $18$ and $20$, and of
DS01, units $1$ to $6$, are used. The operating point is held fixed by
clustering flight points, by $k$-means with $6$ clusters, on standardised
altitude, Mach number and throttle-resolver angle; life is divided into $2$
stages of equal width in cycles. A (stage, cluster) cell enters only with at
least $400$ points.

Within a cell each sensor is regressed on standardised remaining life, and
the slope is divided by the standard deviation of that sensor's residual, so
that the vector of slopes is the informative direction at a fixed operating
point rather than a residual after standardisation. The vector is normalised,
and the test compares the logarithms of the absolute values of its
components, centred on their mean. The logarithmic transformation is
essential: under the separable form of Section~\ref{sec:zero}, interpreted
through the pairing of channels with mechanisms in Eq.~\eqref{M-eq:obs}, each
component is the product of an operating-point factor and a state factor, so
in logarithms the two act additively, and the absence of interaction is what
the two assumptions jointly predict. A sensor map that mixes mechanisms with
weights depending on the operating point violates this additivity just as a
non-separable stress factor does. In N-CMAPSS the operating history enters
the damage through the onset of abnormal
degradation~\cite{arias2022fusing}, and the health parameters supplied with
the files, which are the damage imposed by the simulator, are constant within
every one of the $446$ flights of DS02 and $553$ of DS01. The damage
therefore does not respond to the operating point within a flight, and the
sensor map is the only possible source of any dependence on the operating
point that the test detects.

Each cell is estimated twice, from its even and from its odd points. The mean
of the two estimates is the signal; their half-difference measures the
noise, and applying the same decomposition to it gives each effect its own
noise floor. The decomposition is the ordinary two-way one over the cells,
into a grand mean, a stage effect, an operating-point effect and an
interaction, with sums of squares taken over cells and sensors and pooled
over units. With $6$ clusters and $2$ stages the operating-point effect and
the interaction each have $5$ degrees of freedom per sensor and the stage
effect $1$, so the reported ratio of interaction to operating-point effect
compares quantities with equal degrees of freedom. That ratio is $1.65$ for
DS02 and $1.84$ for DS01. Against its own noise floor the interaction is $566$ times larger on DS02
and $110$ times on DS01, so it cannot be attributed to noise. The test was
repeated at $3$, $4$, $6$, $8$ and $10$ clusters with $2$ stages, at $6$
clusters with $3$ stages and at $4$ clusters with $4$ stages, with at least
$300$ points per cell; the interaction exceeds the operating-point effect in
every configuration, by a factor of at least $1.42$.

\section{Information loss over a record and the best fixed indicator}
\label{sec:proofs}

\subsection{Loss over a complete record}

Let the record consist of measurements \eqref{M-eq:obs} at instants
$t_1,\dots,t_N$, with the noise independent across channels and across
instants, and write $\dvec_k:=\dvec(t_k)$ and $\psi_k$ for the angle between
$\dhat_k$ and $\mathbf v$. Put
\[
G:=\Var[R]\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|\dvec_k\|^{2},\qquad W:=\frac{G}{1+G},\qquad
S:=\frac{\sum_{k}\|\dvec_k\|^{2}\sin^{2}\psi_k}{\sum_{k}\|\dvec_k\|^{2}} .
\]
Then the information about $R$ that the fixed indicator discards over the
complete record is
\begin{equation}
\lloss_{\mathrm{rec}}=-\tfrac12\log\big(1-W S\big).
\label{eq:record}
\end{equation}
In whitened coordinates the linearised record is $\tilde y_k=\dvec_k R+\epsilon_k$ with $\epsilon_k\sim N(0,I_d)$ independent, so the Fisher information it carries about $R$ is $\sum_k\dvec_k^{\top}\dvec_k=\sum_k\|\dvec_k\|^{2}$ and an estimator using the complete record obtains $\tfrac12\log(1+G)$ nats. The fixed indicator returns the scalars $z_k=\mathbf v^{\top}\tilde y_k=(\mathbf v^{\top}\dvec_k)R+\mathbf v^{\top}\epsilon_k$, whose noise terms $\mathbf v^{\top}\epsilon_k$ are standard normal and independent because $\|\mathbf v\|=1$, so the compressed record carries Fisher information $\sum_k(\mathbf v^{\top}\dvec_k)^{2}$. Since $\mathbf v^{\top}\dvec_k=\|\dvec_k\|\cos\psi_k$,
\[
\Var[R]\sum_k(\mathbf v^{\top}\dvec_k)^{2}
=\Var[R]\sum_k\|\dvec_k\|^{2}\cos^{2}\psi_k=G(1-S),
\]
and subtracting the two mutual informations gives $\tfrac12\log(1+G)-\tfrac12\log\big(1+G(1-S)\big)=-\tfrac12\log\frac{1+G-GS}{1+G}=-\tfrac12\log(1-WS)$.

Equation \eqref{eq:record} has the form of \eqref{M-eq:exact}, with the
instantaneous signal-to-noise ratio replaced by its sum over the record and
$\sin^{2}\psi$ by its mean weighted by the signal energy. The averaging,
however, differs: the time-averaged loss \eqref{M-eq:objective} averages the
loss, whereas \eqref{eq:record} averages $\sin^{2}\psi$ inside it, and the
two differ and select different indicators. Minimising \eqref{eq:record} over
$\|\mathbf v\|=1$ maximises $\mathbf v^{\top}\big(\sum_k\dvec_k\dvec_k^{\top}\big)\mathbf v$,
so the best fixed indicator for the record is the leading eigenvector of the
integrated Fisher matrix.

\subsection{The best fixed indicator as a barycentre}

Write $p_i=\dhat_i^{2}$ and $q_i=\mathbf v_i^{2}$ for the information shares.
By \ref{M-app:rotation}, the components of $\dot{\dhat}$ are
$\dhat_i(\dot g_i-\E_p[\dot g])$, so $\dot p_i=2\dhat_i\dot{\dhat}_i$ gives the
replicator equation of evolutionary dynamics,
$\dot p_i=2p_i(\dot g_i-\E_p[\dot g])$~\cite{hofbauer1998}. Moreover,
$\cos\psi=\sum_i\sqrt{p_iq_i}$ is the Bhattacharyya coefficient of $p$
and $q$, $\psi$ is half the Fisher--Rao distance between
them~\cite{amari2016}, and with
$\DB=-\log\sum_i\sqrt{p_iq_i}$ the loss reads
$\lloss=-\tfrac12\log[(1-w)+w\,e^{-2\DB}]$. Let $q^{\star}$ minimise the
time-averaged loss. (i) As $\gamma\to\infty$, $\lloss\to\DB$ and $q^{\star}$
tends to the Bhattacharyya barycentre of the share path. (ii) As the path
shortens, $\lloss=\tfrac{w}{2}\psi^{2}+O(\psi^{4})$ and $q^{\star}$ tends to
the $w$-weighted Fisher--Rao barycentre.

For (i), as $\gamma\to\infty$ we have $w\to1$, and \eqref{M-eq:exact} gives
\[
\lloss\to-\tfrac12\log\cos^{2}\psi=-\log\sum_i\sqrt{p_iq_i}=\DB .
\]
The convergence is uniform, and it is this uniformity that carries over to
the minimisers. Positivity keeps $p_\tau$ interior, and $\sqrt{q_i}\ge q_i$
with $\sum_iq_i=1$ gives $\cos\psi=\sum_i\sqrt{p_iq_i}\ge\min_i\sqrt{p_i}$ for
every $q$ in the simplex, so $\cos\psi$ is bounded away from zero uniformly in
$q$ and in $\tau$ and $\DB$ remains finite. Both objectives are continuous on
a compact set and uniformly close on it, so their minimisers converge. For
(ii), $\bar\lloss=\tfrac12\bar w\,\E_\mu[\psi^{2}]+O(\psi^{4})$ with
$d\mu=w\,d\tau/\bar w$; minimising a $\mu$-weighted mean square of a geodesic
distance is the definition of the weighted Fr\'echet mean, and
$\psi=\tfrac12\dFR$ converts $\tfrac12\bar w\,\E_\mu[\psi^{2}]$ into
$\tfrac18\bar w\,\E_\mu[\dFR^{2}]$.

Either barycentre of a nominal degradation path therefore provides a
suitable initial indicator, and both lie near the midpoint of the arc used in
the bound \eqref{M-eq:arcbound}.

\section{Numerical verification}
\label{sec:verification}

\subsection{Verification of the identities}

The analytical results of the main text were verified numerically before
they were used. The rotation identity \eqref{M-eq:repl} holds to
relative error below $1.1\times10^{-7}$ over the five input--channel pairs
$(d,m)=(2,1)$, $(3,2)$, $(3,1)$, $(4,2)$ and $(5,3)$; the replicator form to
$6.9\times10^{-8}$; the rate of change of the misalignment to $5.1\times10^{-6}$, limited by
finite differencing; and the Bhattacharyya form of the loss to $1.1\times10^{-16}$.

\subsection{Non-generic systems that attain zero loss with a single input}

Consider exponential shapes $h_i(x)=e^{\nu_i(x_i-x_i^0)}$ and Arrhenius
factors written in reciprocal temperature, $\varphi_i(u)=e^{\theta_i-E_iu}$
with $u=1/(kT)$. Under the ramp $u(t)=u_0+rt$ the damage rates are constant,
$x_i(t)=x_i^0+\varphi_i^0t$ with $\varphi_i^0=\varphi_i(u_0)$, so $a(x_t)$
moves at constant velocity $\nu_i\varphi_i^0$. Choosing
$\nu_i=rE_i/\varphi_i^0$ makes that velocity parallel to $E$, which is the
direction of $\mathcal B$ (Section~\ref{sec:zero}), and $b(u_t)$ recedes along $\mathcal B$ at the same
rate. The two effects cancel, and the informative direction remains fixed
with a single input, for any number of mechanisms.

Integrating this construction
for $d=2$ to $8$ with one input holds the misalignment below
$1.5\times10^{-6}$ degrees and the mean loss below $10^{-15}$, against
$1.2\times10^{-5}$ to $5.4\times10^{-4}$ for the best constant input on the
same system. These families have no prior variance of their own, so their losses are evaluated at a fixed $w=0.9$ rather than at a value of $\gamma$ supplied by the system; the bound has a margin of fifteen orders of magnitude and does not depend on this choice.

The family is not robust. Perturbing the shape coefficients $\nu$ by $1\%$ raises the mean loss to
$3.2\times10^{-4}$ under the myopic policy, against $1.8\times10^{-4}$ for the
best constant input on the same perturbed system. With an error of one percent
in the shape coefficients, steering therefore already performs worse than
constant operation. Closing the loop halves this deterioration but does not
remove it, so the fragility is inherent in the construction rather than in
the open-loop schedule. Errors in the activation energies, by contrast, are
tolerated up to about two percent.

\section{Conditions for zero loss and further results on the test system}
\label{sec:further}

\subsection{Conditions for zero loss}
\label{sec:zero}

Zero loss constrains the trajectory severely. If $\dhat$ is held equal to
$\mathbf v$, then $\dot x=\Sigma^{1/2}\dvec=\|\dvec\|\,\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf v$,
so the state can advance only along the straight segment
\begin{equation}
\mathcal S=\{x_0+s\,\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf v:\ 0\le s\le s_f\}
\label{eq:segment}
\end{equation}
from the initial state to the condemnation limit, and the operating point
determines only the speed of progression. Zero loss is therefore attainable
if and only if every state on $\mathcal S$ admits an admissible input that
aligns the indicator, that is, $\mathbf v\in\mathcal D(x)$ for all
$x\in\mathcal S$, with $\mathcal D(x)$ the set \eqref{M-eq:reachset}. The
condition must be verified on $\mathcal S$ itself: an indicator may be
reachable at every state of some other trajectory and nevertheless be
unattainable, because the input that aligns it generates a different
trajectory (Section~\ref{sec:counter} gives a two-state example).

Three consequences follow.
\begin{itemize}
\item Alignment at a given state imposes $d-1$ scalar equations on $m$
unknowns. With fewer than $d-1$ independent operating variables,
$\mathcal D(x_0)$ is a null set, and almost every indicator is already
misaligned at the initial state.
\item With $m\ge d-1$ the alignment equations can be solved locally, but the
solution must also remain within $\mathcal U$ along the whole of
$\mathcal S$. The counting condition is thus necessary for generic data but
not sufficient.
\item Maintaining the misalignment at a constant, non-zero level is a single
scalar condition on $\dot u$ and requires only one operating variable.
\end{itemize}
Systems for which zero loss is attainable with fewer inputs exist for any
number of mechanisms, but they are non-generic: an error of one percent in
their shape coefficients makes steering worse than constant operation
(Section~\ref{sec:verification}).

When the stress factors are separable, $\log\dvec=a(x)+b(u)$ up to a common
constant, with $a_i=\log(h_i/\sigma_i)$ and $b_i=\log\varphi_i$. Every
reachable set is then the translate by $a(x)$ of a single fixed set
$\mathcal B=b(\mathcal U)$, and zero loss requires the image of $\mathcal S$
under $a$ to lie within a single translate of $-\mathcal B$. The actuation
authority $\mathcal B$ can then be computed offline from an accelerated-life
model, without any degradation record; Section~\ref{M-sec:data} of the main
text shows that this form, interpreted through the channel pairing
\eqref{M-eq:obs}, fails on raw turbofan sensors.

\emph{Proof of the conditions.} Let each $h_i$ be locally Lipschitz. By
\eqref{M-eq:exact}, $\bar\lloss=0$ if and only if $\dhat(x_t,u_t)=\mathbf v$ for
almost every $t$. Then $\dot x_t=\|\dvec_t\|\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf v$ almost
everywhere, so $x_t=x_0+s(t)\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf v$ with
$s(t)=\int_0^t\|\dvec_\tau\|d\tau$ continuous and strictly increasing: the
trajectory runs along $\mathcal S$. Since $\mathbf v\in\mathcal D(x_t)$ for
almost every $t$, it lies in $\mathcal D(x)$ for $x$ in a dense subset of
$\mathcal S$, and the set $\{x:\mathbf v\in\mathcal D(x)\}$ is closed because
$\mathcal U$ is compact and $\dhat$ continuous; it therefore contains all of
$\mathcal S$. Conversely, if $\mathbf v\in\mathcal D(x)$ on all of
$\mathcal S$, the map
$s\mapsto\{u\in\mathcal U:\dhat(x_0+s\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf v,u)=\mathbf v\}$ has
non-empty compact values and a closed graph, hence a measurable selection
$\bar u(s)$~\cite[Thm.~8.1.3]{aubin2009}. As $\|\dvec\|$ is continuous and
positive on the compact set $\mathcal S\times\mathcal U$, the time change
$t(s)=\int_0^{s}\|\dvec(x_0+\sigma\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf v,\bar u(\sigma))\|^{-1}
d\sigma$ is finite and strictly increasing, and with $s(t)$ its inverse the
input $u(t)=\bar u(s(t))$ drives \eqref{M-eq:plant} along $\mathcal S$ with
$\psi=0$ up to the condemnation limit; local Lipschitz continuity of $h$
ensures that this is the trajectory the input generates. For $m<d-1$,
$\mathcal D(x_0)$ is the image of the $m$-dimensional compact set
$\mathcal U$ under a continuously differentiable map into the
$(d-1)$-dimensional sphere, hence a null set that almost every $\mathbf v$
misses. In the separable case, $\mathbf v\in\mathcal D(x)$ reads
$\kappa-a(x)\in\mathcal B$ with $\kappa$ the class of $\log\mathbf v$ modulo
the all-ones vector, and the condition on $\mathcal S$ is
$a(\mathcal S)\subseteq\kappa-\mathcal B$.

\subsection{Effect of the time-shift assumption}

Equation~\eqref{M-eq:sens} is exact if and only if the population is spread along a single path. For the system of Section~\ref{M-sec:testplant}, with units instead generated by dispersing the degradation coefficients, the direction returned by the population regression of $x$ on $R$ lies about $45^{\circ}$ from $-\varphi(u)h(x)$, across two families of dispersed coefficients and every spread examined; a control whose units are started on the reference trajectory, where \eqref{M-eq:sens} holds by construction, returns the same regression to within $0.2^{\circ}$. The misalignments considered in this paper amount to a few degrees. Extending the construction to populations dispersed in $\theta$, for which the observations carry scatter that no value of $R$ explains, remains an open problem.

\subsection{Loss at equal Fisher information of the record}

Equation~\eqref{M-eq:fisherpair} can be illustrated on this system, at the
steering indicator $q^{\star}$ of Section~\ref{M-sec:case}. For a
constant input, a common gain on $\dvec$ changes $\|\dvec\|$ without rotating
$\dhat(\tau)$, so the score of the record can be equalised across inputs
while the misalignment remains determined by the input. Let $\delta(u)$ denote
the gain that equalises the score, defined by
$\delta(u)^{2}\,\overline{\|\dvec\|^{2}}(u)=F$ with $F$ its value at
$u=1.5$. It is realised by rescaling the duration for which the component
operates under load, which is why it does not rotate $\dhat$. By
construction $\delta=1$ at $u=1.5$, and $\delta>1$ wherever the raw score is
smaller, so it is a multiplier on exposure rather than a fraction of it.
Table~\ref{tab:levelset} holds the score of the record equal to
seven digits by tuning the exposure factor $\delta$. The loss still spans a factor
$9.4$. The information of the indicator itself, averaged in the same way,
orders the five inputs exactly as the loss does, as \eqref{M-eq:fisherpair}
predicts.

\subsection{An indicator reachable along a trajectory but unattainable on the segment}
\label{sec:counter}

Let $d=2$, $m=1$, unit noise, $\varphi_i(u)=e^{\theta_i-E_iu}$ with
$\theta=(0,0.5)$, $E=(1,2)$ and $\mathcal U=[0,1.3]$, shapes $h_1=1$ and
$h_2(x)=e^{x}$, $x_0=0$, $x_f=0.9$, and the indicator with
$\log(\mathbf v_2/\mathbf v_1)=-0.2$. It is aligned at state $x$ by the input
$u=0.7+x_2$, which is admissible exactly when $x_2\le0.6$. Under the constant
input $u=1.3$ the system fails with $x_2=0.52$, so the indicator is reachable at
every state that this trajectory visits. Zero loss is nevertheless
unattainable: along the segment \eqref{eq:segment}, $x_2=e^{-0.2}x_1$, and $x_2$ reaches $0.6$ at
$x_1=0.73$, before failure. The condition of Section~\ref{sec:zero} must
therefore be verified on the segment rather than on the trajectory generated
by an arbitrary fixed input.

\subsection{Attainability of zero loss with one and two inputs}
\label{sec:floor}

With a single input, no direction lies in every reachable set of the test
system. Along the closed loop at the steering indicator $q^{\star}$ the floor
is largest at the start of life, where $\beta_{\min}=4.52^{\circ}$; it
decreases through mid-life to below the $0.01^{\circ}$ resolution of the
sampling, where the indicator is momentarily reachable, and increases again
towards failure (Fig.~\ref{fig:floor}(a)). Zero loss with $q^{\star}$ would
require $q^{\star}$ to be reachable at every point of its segment
$\mathcal S$, including $x_0$, where the floor is largest; it is therefore
unattainable under any input history. Fig.~\ref{fig:main}(a) illustrates the
geometry.

A second independent input is introduced by setting
$\varphi_i(u)=\exp(\theta_i-E_iu_1-G_iu_2)$ with $G=(0.9,0.3,1.2)$, so that
$m=2=d-1$. Then $\log\dvec_i$ is affine in $(u_1,u_2)$, and matching a target
direction amounts to two linear equations in two unknowns, with a coefficient
matrix of determinant $0.27$ here, and hence a unique solution at every
state. The target is taken as the direction occupied by the system at
mid-life under the central operating point $(1.5,1.5)$ of the box. Zero loss
with this indicator confines the state to $\mathcal S$, and at every one of
$600$ points of that segment the aligning input lies inside the admissible
box $[0,3]^{2}$, at least $0.52$ from its boundary. Along the segment it
sweeps $u_1$ from $0.55$ to $2.48$ while $u_2$ stays between $1.32$ and
$1.70$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:floor}(b)). Under this feedback the state remains on
$\mathcal S$ to machine precision and the misalignment below $10^{-6}$
degrees, that is, zero to numerical precision, until failure. An independent
$90\times90$ grid search over the box along the segment returns a floor of at
most $0.152^{\circ}$, which reflects the resolution of the grid rather than a
property of the system. For this system, therefore, the addition of one
operating variable reduces the peak floor from $4.52^{\circ}$ to zero, and
zero loss is attained along the entire closed loop.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the condemnation limit}

Raising $x_f$ to unity leaves every qualitative conclusion unchanged and
alters the constants as follows: the peak floor of Section~\ref{sec:floor} rises from
$4.52^{\circ}$ to $5.41^{\circ}$, and the factor of
Section~\ref{M-sec:case} at the steering indicator falls from $68$ to $58$.
The constants reported in the main text therefore apply only at the stated
condemnation limit.

\subsection{Computed fractional optimum}
\label{sec:frac}

Dinkelbach's transform~\cite{dinkelbach1967} converts the fractional problem
\eqref{M-eq:frac} into a sequence of exit-time problems whose value functions
solve a stationary Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman equation on the state alone;
these are discretised by a monotone semi-Lagrangian
scheme~\cite{falcone2014}. The discrete Dinkelbach problem at $q^{\star}$ is solved on state grids of
$25$ to $137$ points per axis, with the control grid of the policy. The
scheme uses a fixed spatial step rather than a fixed time step, because
degradation speeds vary by orders of magnitude across $\mathcal U$; since the
drift is positive, the interpolation cell of every foot point has all its
corners other than the node itself at a larger index sum, so the discrete
equation is solved exactly by one sweep in decreasing index sum, and the
optimal ratio by Dinkelbach's own iteration. The values
converge at first order in the mesh width: fitted to the four finest grids,
the order is $0.99$. Extrapolated from those four,
$\lambda^{\star}_\infty=1.65\times10^{-4}$. The myopic policy attains
$1.68\times10^{-4}$ and is realisable, so it bounds the optimum from above,
and the optimum lies between the two values. The myopic policy therefore sits
$1.4\%$ above the optimum as extrapolated. Fitting the four next-finest grids instead, or
repeating the computation on control grids of $81$ and $161$ points, puts the
gap between $1.4\%$ and $2.0\%$, while refining the control grid from $41$ to
$81$ points lowers both the myopic value and the extrapolated optimum by more
than $4\%$.

For this system, therefore, the fractional form of \eqref{M-eq:frac} affects
the formulation of the problem and its certification, but not measurably its
solution: a policy that considers only the numerator is optimal to within the
resolution of the computation. The lower end of the interval rests on the
extrapolation and is not a bound, since even the finest grid returns a value $27\%$ above it. The
computed value is subject to three checks: the Dinkelbach function must
vanish at the computed $\lambda^{\star}$, $\lambda^{\star}$ must not exceed
the value of a realisable policy, and the grid sequence must extrapolate
consistently.

\subsection{Screening by actuation authority and drift}
\label{sec:regime}

Whether steering can reduce the loss substantially can be assessed from the
system model before any indicator is designed. Across $299$ random systems of the separable model, the reachable-set width
ranks the systems according to whether steering cuts the loss fivefold with an
area under the receiver-operating curve of $0.73$; the drift of the
informative direction contributes additional information only through its
interaction with the width, as the rest of this section shows.

Let $\rho$ denote the drift divided by the length, which weighs the two against each other.

Table~\ref{tab:regime} varies the spread of the activation energies, which by

attainable loss $L^{\star}$ rises by a factor $25$ and the value of control

Three systems that differ only in their activation energies do not establish
a screening rule, so the index was tested on $299$ further systems drawn at random: two to
five mechanisms, one or two independently adjustable inputs, and the
pre-exponentials, activation energies, shape coefficients, input envelope,
initial state and signal-to-noise ratio varied together. The gain is measured
as in the first gain column of Table~\ref{tab:regime}, against the best
constant input at the steering indicator, which on the three systems of that
table overstates it by factors of $1.3$ to $1.4$ relative to a constant design
with its own indicator. The rank correlation between $\rho$ and the gain is $-0.36$, with a
bootstrap $95\%$ interval from $-0.46$ to $-0.24$, and it keeps its sign inside
every stratum of the mechanism count and of the input count, so it is not an
artefact of either.

Used as a classifier of whether steering reduces the loss fivefold, an
outcome observed for $32\%$ of the systems, $\rho$ has an area under the receiver-operating curve
of $0.73$, with interval $0.67$ to $0.79$. The reachable arc alone scores
$0.73$ as well, and the drift alone $0.53$, with interval $0.46$ to $0.60$,
which includes the value $0.5$ of a random classifier.

Equal areas do not imply equal merit, and the question is resolved by a
model that contains both as special cases: a logistic regression on the
logarithms of arc and drift as separate terms, of which the arc alone and the
ratio $\rho$ are each a constrained case. The drift adds nothing to the arc as
a main effect ($p=0.47$), and constraining the two to enter as a ratio fits
significantly worse than the arc alone ($p=7.7\times10^{-5}$; Akaike
information $351$ against $336$). Both results hold in each input count
separately. The drift does carry information, but through the arc
($p=3.0\times10^{-4}$ for their interaction), and in opposite directions at the
two ends. Among systems of low authority the share repaying fivefold falls from
$0.18$ to $0.08$ as the drift rises from its lowest to its highest third; among
systems of high authority it rises from $0.48$ to $0.61$. Drift that exceeds
the authority defeats steering, whereas drift that the authority can follow
is what makes steering useful. The ratio captures only the first of these
effects. This also explains why the lowest tenth of $\rho$ repays fivefold only $43\%$ of the time:
there the drift is small, a median of $2.7^{\circ}$ against $14^{\circ}$ over
the sample.

The ranking is confirmed out of sample. Over repeated ten-fold
cross-validation the arc and $\rho$ each rank unseen systems with an area of $0.72$, and a model
adding the drift and its interaction with the arc reaches $0.74$, higher in
every repeat. The appropriate screening quantity is therefore the arc, that
is, the authority itself, refined by the drift only through their
interaction; $\rho$ remains a convenient description of the family of
Table~\ref{tab:regime}, in which the drift is nearly fixed. The first row of
that table is not typical of its own regime: among sampled systems within a
factor $1.4$ of its $\rho$, the median gain is
$5.2$ rather than $68$.

\subsection{Effect of the loss on a downstream estimator}
\label{sec:downstream}

Equation~\eqref{M-eq:mmse} expresses the loss in terms of the error of the estimate prescribed by the model itself. Since a practitioner does not use that estimate, the ordering is examined against a procedure that uses none of the construction: the indicator is sampled over the last $40\%$ of life, the observed history is matched against the indicator's own reference curve, and the remaining life is read from the best-fitting time offset. This estimator uses only the scalar signal; it has no access to $\dhat$, $\gamma$ or the loss. It is the estimator of Table~\ref{M-tab:policy}.

On one constant-input path of the test system, over twelve indicators spanning $\bar\lloss$ from $0.012$ to $0.37$, its median absolute error, one of the standard prognostic accuracy measures~\cite{saxena2008metrics}, increases with the loss, at Spearman correlation $0.89$, and the best-aligned indicator beats the worst by a factor $1.5$. Since twelve indicators constitute a small sample, both quantities are reported with intervals obtained by resampling the $160$ units behind each median: $0.34$ to $0.90$ for the correlation, and $1.40$ to $2.03$ for the factor. The ordering is statistically significant (a one-sided permutation test gives $p=1.5\times10^{-4}$), but its strength is uncertain, and the point estimate lies near the upper end of its own interval. The factor is far smaller than the $31$-fold spread in $\bar\lloss$, as expected: the loss is a logarithmic information measure, whereas the error is a time, and an estimator using the whole history recovers much of what any single
instant discards.

The same protocol on $120$ further systems, drawn at random as in
Section~\ref{sec:regime}, gives correlations between $0.87$ and $0.99$, with
the system above among the weaker. That design, however, tilts every indicator
towards the same direction, so that loss and error both increase with a
single parameter and their agreement is largely structural. Tilting instead
by a random amount of up to $60^{\circ}$ in a random direction weakens the
median correlation to
$0.74$, with the interval excluding zero on $84\%$ of systems. It also reveals
a limitation of the experiment. On these systems the angle between an
indicator and the mid-life informative direction ranks the indicators
identically to the loss on $82\%$ of systems, with neither consistently better
on the remainder, because the direction rotates little compared with the
differences between the indicators. The experiment is therefore evidence that
the ordering of the loss carries over to an estimator built from none of this
construction; it is not evidence that the time-averaged loss improves on a
mid-life snapshot of the misalignment, which would require a system whose
direction rotates by more than its candidate indicators differ.
